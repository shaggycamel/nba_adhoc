Predicting a player's usage before tip-off

Can we forecast how big a share of his team's possessions a player will use in his next game? We tested simple averages, injury information, teammate absences, role models and nine model types on identical time-based splits.

Scope: the target is usage in games where the player plays at least 10 minutes. Whether a player plays at all is not modelled. Injury information exists only from the 2021-22 season.

1 · What was predicted, and how it was tested

The target is usg_pct from the player box score: the share of team possessions a player uses while on court (0.20 means 20%). For each player-game we predict it using only what was known before tip-off. Every rolling statistic is computed from games strictly before the target game; the injury report is the pre-game report for that date, never who actually played.

Time-based folds (expanding window)

Train on every earlier season, test on the next one. Models were tuned on the three validation folds only; the holdout was never used to choose anything.

Feature groups

2 · Data processing: how to average a player's past usage

Before any model, we compared single-number predictors on the same validation folds. Averaging windows, decay rates and how history is cleaned all matter at the 0.0005 level, which is about the size of the gap between a model and a good average.

These choices were made on the same validation folds as the models, so they are mildly optimistic. The holdout shows the same ordering. We tested missing history by falling back from season mean to career mean to the league mean for players with little history; rows with fewer than 5 prior games are about 1% of test rows but show the largest improvement from the model (see section 7). {{return_note}}

3 · Which model wins?

Nine model types (Ridge, ElasticNet, random forest, extra trees, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, an MLP and a GRU) were each tuned on the same three validation folds (a small grid per model) and then scored on the 2025-26 holdout. Lower is better; the axis does not start at zero, because the differences are small.

Differences between the three boosting libraries are within noise (confidence intervals include zero). The ensemble that averages seven models is better than any single one by a tiny but measurable amount ({{ens_gain}} MAE), which is not worth the added complexity on its own. 4 · Loss functions and training

The headline metric is mean absolute error (MAE), the average size of the miss in usage points. We also report RMSE (penalises large misses more) and R² (share of variance explained). The models were trained with a choice of loss function:

Squared error (L2)
minimises the mean of (prediction − actual)². Targets the mean; sensitive to outliers. Default for Ridge, forests and the boosting libraries.
Absolute error (L1)
minimises the mean of |prediction − actual|. Targets the median and matches the MAE metric directly.
Huber (δ = 0.05)
squared error for misses under 0.05 usage points, absolute error beyond, so rare extreme games (a star leaving injured after three minutes) do not dominate. Used for the final neural networks.
Fair (c = 0.05)
a smooth robust loss between L1 and L2.

The neural nets predict usage as the player's EWMA plus a learned correction, are trained with AdamW and a one-cycle learning-rate schedule for 12 epochs, and use a single seed. The MLP has 16-dimensional player embeddings (players with 30 or more training games). The GRU reads each player's previous 20 games.

5 · Does injury information matter?

We tested two things explicitly: the player's own report status (Available, Probable, Questionable, Doubtful, Out) and the absence of teammates. For teammates we used only players on that day's report, weighted by how much usage they normally carry, and separated fresh absences (the player appeared in the last 14 days) from stale ones (already baked into everyone's recent usage).

Only 127 player-games had an "Out" report but the player played anyway, so the own-status result is also limited by sample size. The chart above compares each player's actual usage to his last-10 average, which is why the baseline is slightly negative.

Minutes-weighted EWMA features

Since a minutes-weighted EWMA was the best simple average, we added three variants to the full model as features. Paired change in MAE: {{pr_diff}} (95% CI {{pr_lo}} to {{pr_hi}}). The model already contains minutes-weighted rolling usage, so these add little.

Files that were not used as features, and why

  • statyx/usage_shock: each row was calculated after the fact (calculated_at in September 2026) over windows that run to June 2026. Using it for earlier games would leak the future.
  • statyx/play_types, shot_zones, shot_zone_defense, play_type_defense, defense_vs_position, standings: single end-of-season or later snapshots for one season. They cannot be dated to before each game, and there is no earlier history to train on.
  • statyx/potential_assists covers 2025-26 only; statyx/contracts has two seasons. Neither has enough history to train and test on separately.
  • util/player_id_map_vw was used to join the two sources; nba/player_info, teams and team_roster supplied team names for matching injury reports.

9 · Method details and caveats

  • Rows: {{rows}} player-games with at least 10 minutes and at least one prior game, 2013-14 to 2025-26, NBA regular season and playoffs only. {{rows_inj}} of them are in the injury-report era (2021-22 onward); injury features are missing earlier and the models handle that natively (trees) or with an indicator (linear and neural).
  • Significance: paired differences in absolute error with 95% confidence intervals from a game-level bootstrap (1,000 resamples), which assumes games are independent of each other.
  • Tuning: small grids per model family, selected by mean MAE on the three validation folds. The holdout is shown alongside but never used to choose. Processing choices in section 2 used the same folds.
  • Role clustering: k-means (k = 6) on prior-game rate statistics excluding usage, refit on each fold's training data only. It was kept in the model comparison for completeness, but ablations show it adds nothing once injury features are present.
  • Limits: the model predicts usage given that the player plays 10 or more minutes; it does not predict whether a player plays. Injury reports are taken as published before the game, but real reports change; 127 games had an "Out" report and the player played. Neural networks used a single seed. Differences below about 0.0001 MAE should not be interpreted.
