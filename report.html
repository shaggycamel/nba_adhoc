Forecasting next-game minutes and usage

The winning combination is a regularised linear model on lagged usage history plus injury-absence and rotation-position features. It beats the best baseline by about 4% on MAE and adds roughly 0.05 to R², consistently across both validation seasons.

That is a real gain and a modest one. The honest summary is that usage is mostly autocorrelated and only slightly contextual: an exponentially weighted mean of a player's recent usage gets most of the way there, and everything else is a small correction on top.

How this was measured. Expanding-window season folds — train on every earlier season, score the next one. Nothing is shuffled. Every feature is computed from games strictly before the one being predicted, and usage is modelled conditional on the player appearing.

Minutes is the model worth having

Minutes on 2025-26, for a backup playing while a starter-minutes teammate is ruled out. This is the case the project exists for.

Modelling takes minutes from 5.901 MAE, R² 0.251 for the best recent average to 3.823, R² 0.295. That nearly doubles explained variance — far more than the usage model gains over its own baseline, and the reason minutes rather than usage is the deliverable.

It carries through to scoring: points on the same slice go from 4.025 MAE (R² 0.184) to 3.825 (R² 0.289). Volume is projected as predicted minutes times a predicted per-minute rate, with the rate model given an out-of-fold usage prediction.

The player's own injury status is worth nothing — 0.04978 against 0.04978 for no injury features at all. On reflection that is what should happen: the target only exists for games the player actually played, so conditioning on their having played already absorbs almost everything their status could say. Own status would matter for predicting whether someone plays, which is a different question.

Teammate absence matters mainly through rotation position, not through volume. Knowing how much usage was vacated is worth about 0.0003 MAE; knowing where that pushes the player in the healthy rotation is worth about as much again, the single largest structural contribution. What counts is not how much load went missing but how far up the pecking order it moves this player.

What failed

dropped Role clustering

K-means over prior-game rate stats with usage excluded, refitted each season on earlier seasons only, feeding role-aware vacated load. It changed MAE by at most 0.00002 in either direction. The project rule is that clusters are kept only on a demonstrated gain, so it is out of the recommended set.

dropped Pairwise with/without absorption

Shrunk estimates of how each player's usage shifts when each specific teammate is out. The individual estimates look sensible — the strongest pair is +7.7 usage points over 42 games-without — but once rotation share is in the model the pairwise detail is redundant. Teammate-specific synergy, measured this way, does not forecast usage.

dropped Neural networks

The MLP with player embeddings and the GRU over each player's last ten games were the two worst models tested, behind every classical algorithm. The sequence model was worst of all, which says the ordering of recent games carries nothing beyond their weighted average.

The pattern is the finding. Everything that failed was a re-expression of information already in the player's own rolling history. The one block that replicated describes something that history structurally cannot contain: what the organisation wants. A team above the playoff line in March behaves differently from one out of the race, and no amount of a player's past box scores reveals it.

Caveats

  • Injury data starts 2021-10-19, so every injury-aware result uses five seasons, not the seventeen the box scores cover.
  • 10,160 of 64,246 injury rows are dropped because they do not match a game for that team on that date. The NBA publishes the report the evening before, so some rows carry the report date rather than the game date. This understates absences; it cannot leak. Recovering them is the first thing to try if the absence features are worth pushing further.
  • Two folds only for the injury-era results. The differences between the top models are smaller than the gap between folds, so read the ranking as "all equivalent" rather than as a winner.
  • 2021-22 is unusable for absence spells — its report matches too few games for consecutive absences to join up, so every spell there reads as one game.
  • Minutes are whole numbers in this dataset. Reconstructing usage from its observed components misses the stored column by 0.0138 MAE, a floor on anything formula-based.
  • Everything is modelled conditional on the player appearing. Projecting someone who might be ruled out needs an availability model first.
